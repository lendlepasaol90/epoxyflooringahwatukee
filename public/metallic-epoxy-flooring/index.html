Metallic epoxy floors with real depth

Metallic epoxy is part coating, part craft. Pigments suspended in clear resin are moved by hand while the floor is wet, so it cures with drift, depth and shimmer that change as you walk across the room.

People usually find metallic floors through a photo: a living room that looks like it has a slab of polished stone for a floor, or a garage that could pass for a car showroom. In person, the effect is even stronger, because the reflective pigments catch light differently as you move.

That uniqueness is the whole appeal, and it is also the thing to understand before you choose it. A metallic floor cannot be matched to a photo exactly. We control the palette, the direction of movement and the overall character, but the resin and pigments do the final work as they settle. If you want a floor that is unmistakably yours, that is a feature. If you need a perfectly uniform surface, a solid color or flake system will suit you better.

How a metallic floor is made

Metallic pigments are fine mica particles coated in metal oxides. Mixed into a clear epoxy, they float and reflect light. When the resin is poured and spread, we work it with rollers, trowels, brushes and sometimes air or solvent sprays to push the pigments into swirls, veins, lava-like drifts or soft clouds.

As the resin levels out, the pigments continue to move slightly and settle. The result has real visual depth, because you are looking into a layer of resin rather than at a painted surface.

The process depends on a few things being right: a flat, well-prepared slab, a dark or tinted primer that sets the background tone, and steady room conditions while the floor levels. Heat and airflow both affect how a metallic floor moves, which is why we control the space carefully during the pour.

Palettes that suit Ahwatukee homes

Any color is possible, but these palettes tend to work with desert architecture and natural light.

Sandstone and pearl

Warm whites and soft tans with pearl highlights. Light and airy, beautiful in living spaces with big windows.

Copper and bronze

Rich warm metals that echo the sunset colors on South Mountain. Striking in game rooms and showcase garages.

Slate and charcoal

Deep greys with silver movement. Modern, calm and a good background for colorful furniture or cars.

Marble-style veining

A light base with fine darker veins worked through it, giving a stone-like look without the cost or cold of real marble.

Metallic floors are wonderful in the right setting. Here is an honest view of what they do well and where they need extra thought.

Our metallic installation process

Metallic work takes more steps than other systems, and most of them happen before any color goes down.

  1. Design conversation

    We look at photos you like, talk palette and movement, and show sample boards. We are honest about what is achievable.

  2. Grind, repair and flatten

    Metallic resin reveals every dip. Cracks and low spots are filled and the slab ground flat.

  3. Primer and tinted base

    A primer seals the concrete, then a tinted base coat sets the background tone the metallics will play against.

  4. Metallic pour and manipulation

    The pigmented resin is poured, spread and worked by hand in a single session to keep the movement continuous.

  5. Protective topcoat

    A clear topcoat in gloss or satin adds scratch and UV resistance suited to the room.

Why metallic floors are not a DIY project

Metallic epoxy is unforgiving. It shows roller marks, uneven mixing, dust that settles during cure and any lap where one batch meets another. The working time is short, especially in a warm Arizona room, and the whole floor has to be poured and worked in one continuous session for the effect to look natural.

Experience matters here more than with any other system we install. It is the difference between a floor that looks like flowing stone and one that looks like a spill. We are always glad to show sample boards and talk through realistic expectations before you commit.

Light, heat and metallic floors in the desert

Arizona light is strong, and metallic floors respond to it. Morning light through east windows brings out warm tones; late-afternoon sun can make a floor almost glow. That is beautiful, but it also means UV exposure. We specify UV-stable topcoats in sunlit rooms so the decorative layer keeps its true color.

Temperature matters during installation too. We control airflow and keep the space at a steady temperature while the metallic layer levels, and in summer that often means starting early and keeping the HVAC running. It is one of several reasons we schedule interior metallic work carefully and do not rush it.

Living with a metallic floor

Day to day, a metallic floor is easy to clean: dust mop, then damp mop with a neutral cleaner. The care is mostly about protecting the gloss. Put felt pads on chair and table legs, use mats at exterior doors to catch grit, and avoid dragging heavy furniture.

Gloss finishes show dust and fine scratches more than satin ones, which is a trade-off for the depth gloss adds to the metallic effect. Over the years, if the topcoat dulls in busy paths, it can be lightly abraded and recoated, bringing the depth back without touching the decorative layer underneath.

What goes into the price

Metallic floors cost more than solid or flake systems because they involve more layers, more material and a skilled pour that cannot be rushed. Size, slab condition, the amount of flattening needed, the number of colors and the topcoat choice all affect the price. We quote each project after seeing the space and discussing the look you want, and we include sample boards so the decision is made with real expectations.

Metallic epoxy questions

Straight answers to what people ask us most before they book.

Can you match the floor in a photo I saw online?

We can match the palette and general style, and we will show you sample boards first. An exact match is not possible, because every metallic floor moves differently as it cures. Most clients come to love that their floor is unique.

Do metallic floors scratch easily?

The topcoat protects the decorative layer. High-gloss finishes show fine scratches from grit more readily than satin, so in busy rooms we often recommend a satin topcoat and good entry mats.

Can metallic go in a garage?

Yes, and it looks striking. It works best in garages used mainly for parking and display rather than heavy workshop use. A tough polyaspartic topcoat and an anti-slip additive are usually part of the system.

How long does a metallic floor take?

Plan on two to four days depending on size and prep, plus cure time before furniture or vehicles. We give you a written schedule with your estimate.

Is metallic epoxy more expensive?

Usually, yes, because it involves more layers, more labor and more skill. Prep and the size of the area still drive most of the cost, so we price it individually after seeing the space.

Can a metallic floor go over a heated or uneven slab?

Uneven slabs are ground and filled first, since metallic resin reveals every dip. Radiant-heated slabs are uncommon here, but if you have one we will review manufacturer guidance for that specific system before quoting.

Can you add a logo or design into a metallic floor?

Simple borders, color zones and some inlaid designs are possible in many projects. Detailed logos are usually better handled as a separate layer under the topcoat. We will review what is practical during the design conversation.
